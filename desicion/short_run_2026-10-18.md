# Short run, finished 2026-10-18

What the short validation run measured once all four learned arms had trained. Run folder:
`~/thesis_runs/short_2026-10-17/` (`run_short.sh`, `STATUS.md`, `short.log`, `summary.md`,
`runs/`, `logs/`). Code `slap-mapd-coupling` `main` at `9b6be93` (PR D and PR E merged).
The first attempt of 2026-10-17, where no arm trained, is in `short_run_2026-10-17.md`. Its
Token Passing numbers are the same as here.

Analysis scripts (not part of the run): the paired comparisons, the cost table and the split
count below were computed from the raw rows with throwaway scripts. The split count ran 8
Token Passing episodes of a few seconds each. Nothing was trained.

## Setup

- The 8-agent warehouse (94 vertices, 10 storage, 4 delivery vertices), horizon 2000. 100 SKUs,
  8 units each, starting stock in blocks. Zipf demand with skew 1, the ranking moves 5 places
  every 200 steps.
- Four learned arms, decentralised, centralised, section (3 zones) and full view, each trained
  10 iterations (140 000 timesteps, 7 runners) under fixed storage at load 0.6, training seed 0.
- Checkpoints at iterations 3, 6, 9 and 10, chosen on 2 validation seeds. The chosen and the
  last checkpoint then played 6 test seeds (200 000 to 200 005) under fixed, demand and
  congestion storage (Δ 100, ν 48, β 3), at load 0.6 and 0.3.
- Token Passing on the same seeds, rules and loads.
- Mean ± standard deviation over the 6 test seeds. Gains are paired seed by seed. "6/6" counts
  the seeds where the paired difference has the stated sign. 95% intervals use the t
  distribution with 5 degrees of freedom, as in the gain test of `sec:pf:measures`.

## Inferred, not measured

- **RQ1 is a yes for every arm that keeps up.** Both adaptive rules raise throughput at load
  0.6 on every seed for the three controllers and Token Passing, and the demand rule shortens
  service time at load 0.3 with an interval above zero for the centralised and section arms and
  Token Passing. The decentralised arm's service time is too noisy to read, and full view does
  not keep up at all.
- **The difference between arms at load 0.3 is mostly the queue, not coordination.** Travel
  saved per task is almost the same for the three learned controllers (3.6 to 3.8). The service
  time gain is larger where the fleet runs closer to its limit (centralised keep-up ratio 0.87,
  gain 57%) than further from it (section 0.91, 52%). The learned arms gain more than Token
  Passing mainly because they travel longer routes (17 to 22 steps against 13), so there is
  more travel to save. A clean RQ2 answer needs a load where waiting for an agent is small, or
  a gain read in travel next to service time.
- **In throughput the gain is about the same for every controller** (12%). Nothing here says
  coordinating more agents makes adaptive storage more or less useful.
- **RQ3 at β 3 is a no for every arm.** The congestion rule does not lower blocked time for
  the learned arms at load 0.3, it raises it, and it adds travel everywhere. This repeats the
  probe. β 3 should not be the long run's value.
- **Moving more agents per decision costs runtime** (6.2, 12.5, 17.6 ms for 1, about 3 and 8
  agents), but this run does not show that it buys anything. Under fixed storage the
  decentralised arm finishes most tasks and the centralised arm fewest, as in the night run.
  After 10 iterations this is a statement about learning speed, not about the arms.
- **Relocation looks expensive against the travel it saves directly.** Each update moves 2.4
  to 8 times more distance than the task travel it saves in the next 100 steps. The value comes
  through the queue, not the saved distance. Whether it pays depends on how relocation is
  carried out, which the simulator leaves free.
- **The cap splits SKUs, so part of each update is wasted.** With the uniform pickup draw a
  SKU split between two shelves sends half of its orders to the old one. Moving whole SKUs
  (all units at once, and every later order goes to the new shelf) would remove the split and
  make each unit moved count fully. See the proposal below.

## Proposal raised by the author on 2026-10-18, whole-SKU relocation

The author suggested that an update move all units of a SKU at once, so every later order for
it goes to the new location. Then measure how much the relocation costs against how many steps
it saves, probably as a graph, and how it adds to the overall result.

What the data says about it.
- It solves problem 2 of `stock_template_2026-10-17.md` (the uniform pickup draw) without
  changing the pickup rule. With starting stock in blocks every SKU is at one vertex, and with
  whole-SKU moves it stays at one vertex, so uniform and unit-proportional draws coincide.
- The cap would count SKUs (or units, moved only in whole SKUs). 48 units are 6 SKUs.
- Each move has a cost, units x d_G(old, new), and a saving that can be attributed to it, the
  orders for that SKU after the move times the change in the expected pickup leg. Both are
  logged per update, so the saving needs no counterfactual run.

How to show the value, two graphs.
1. **Cost against gain, a sweep of the cap.** Evaluate with ν = 0 (fixed), 1, 2, 4, 6 and 12
   SKUs per update, no retraining. x is relocation distance per 100 steps, y is the service
   time gain (and throughput gain at load 0.6), one line per controller. This shows whether
   the gain levels off and what each step of it costs.
2. **Cumulative cost and saving over an episode.** Relocation distance summed over updates,
   next to the travel saved summed over tasks, for one seed per arm. This shows when the moves
   pay back.

Not decided. Needs a model change, logged as an `[Axx]` tag, and a small PR.

**Revised later on 2026-10-18, after the author's point on split inventory.** The thesis lists
split inventory as something the state can represent (`2.Introduction.tex:354`), useful for
keeping some stock near one delivery vertex and some near another. Moving whole SKUs to one shelf
would lose that, so that version is dropped. Today split inventory buys nothing, for three
reasons. The pickup shelf is drawn independently of the order's delivery vertex. Both storage
rules use one demand per SKU and the distance to the nearest delivery vertex, so they never split
on purpose. And stock never runs out, so a pickup that chose the nearest holder would let one
unit near each delivery vertex serve every order there. Two options remain.
- Option 1 (recommended before the long run). Keep split inventory, apply the cap per SKU (a
  SKU's planned change happens in full or not at all), and draw the pickup in proportion to
  units. State as a limitation that shelves are not matched to delivery vertices.
- Option 2 (an extension). Pickup at the holder nearest the order's delivery vertex, a unit
  serves a limited number of picks per storage period, and the storage rules plan per SKU and
  delivery vertex. Changes the coupling, both rules and the tests, and the limit will bind (the
  top SKU gets about 11 orders per 100 steps at load 0.6 against 8 units).

The cost graphs above hold for either option. The research-goal changes that follow are in
`research_goals_2026-10-18.md`.

## The main result (raised and agreed by the author on 2026-10-18)

**Decided 2026-10-18.** The author agreed to the proposal below. Thesis wording is proposed
in chat and not yet applied.

The thesis now asks five research questions plus the commons question, under two loads, three
storage rules, four arms and two training regimes. The author asked to settle one main result.

Proposed main result: **how much adaptive storage shortens service time, and how that gain
depends on how many agents one decision covers.** One comparison (demand storage against
fixed), one measure (the paired gain in mean service time with its 95% interval, explained by
its three parts), one factor (the controller, centralised 8, section about 3, decentralised 1,
with Token Passing as the reference), at one load where every arm keeps up on every seed. It is
RQ1 and RQ2 together, so it narrows the thesis rather than changing it.

Everything else supports it, in one paragraph or one figure each. Throughput at load 0.6 shows
the gain also holds when the fleet falls behind. The cost curve (relocation against gain) shows
what the gain costs. RQ3 (congestion rule) and RQ4 (load) become the limits of the main result.
Full view is a diagnostic. RQ5 only if time allows.

What this run says about it. The gain is clear for three of the four routers. At load 0.3 the
differences between arms are mostly the queue, so the main load should be one where waiting
for an agent is small (0.2 is the candidate), or the result must be read with its split.

Alternatives, not recommended as the main result. The cost curve on its own (an engineering
result, weaker on the coupling question). The controller comparison under fixed storage (not
the thesis' question, and after 10 iterations it reflects learning speed).

## Limits

One training seed, so arm and seed are confounded. 10 iterations, far from trained, with every
arm still improving. 6 test seeds, giving intervals of about ±10 to ±16 steps on the service
time gains. One instance, one setting of the storage rules (Δ 100, ν 48, β 3). Runtime measured
with other jobs on the machine. Every adaptive result uses the uniform pickup draw and the
instant switch, both open in `stock_template_2026-10-17.md`.

## What to change before the long runs

1. **Decide the relocation rule first**, whole SKUs (this note) or unit-proportional pickup
   (stock note). Every adaptive number changes with it.
2. **Budget.** At least 500 000 timesteps. Every arm chose its last checkpoint and was still
   improving.
3. **Training seeds.** At least 3. RQ2 compares arms, and with one seed an arm's result is also
   its seed's.
4. **Test seeds.** 10 to 12. The decentralised service time has a spread of 36 to 48.
5. **Loads.** Add 0.2. At 0.3 the gain is mostly the queue, which mixes how close an arm runs
   to its limit into RQ2. Keep 0.6 for throughput.
6. **Arms.** The three controllers and Token Passing. Full view as a diagnostic at one
   condition, since it does not keep up at 0.3 and its training fell at iteration 10.
7. **Storage rules.** Demand as the main rule. Congestion at β 1 only.
8. **Checkpoint choice** on validation at the measurement load, or say in the thesis that it is
   the training load.
9. **Runtime** in a separate pass, one job alone on the machine.
10. **Matched regime** after the fixed-storage long run, with one arm first.
